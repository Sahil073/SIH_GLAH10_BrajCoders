BrajCoders 1 Design Philosophy– Read This Before Anything Else A dashboard that mirrors sensor values (“HR: 72, SpO2: 98%, Temp: 32 deg C”) is not a product– it is a science-fair demo. The product is the layer of judgment sitting on top of the numbers. Every screen in Sanjeevni must answer exactly one of three questions for the person looking at it: 1. “Am I okay right now?”– status, in plain language, not a number. 2. “What should I do about it?”– one clear, small, doable action. 3. “Who else needs to know?”– escalation to family, caregiver, or emergency services. The Indian-context constraints that shape every decision in this document: ˆ ˆ ˆ ˆ ˆ ˆ Rural internet penetration is 48.31 per 100 vs. 126.80 per 100 in urban India– the app must be fully useful with zero or intermittent connectivity, not just “work offline as a fallback.” The primary at-risk users– elderly citizens and outdoor laborers– are frequently not the most digitally literate members of their household. A family member is often the one who will actually monitor the app. Health literacy varies widely; numbers (SpO2 94%, heat index 41 deg C) mean very little to most users without translation into an action. Multiple Indian languages, and in many cases low reading literacy even in one’s own language, means voice and icons must be first-class, not accessibility add-ons. Trust in unfamiliar health-tech is reasonably low; the app must be transparent about what it is (a risk-alerting companion) and is not (a diagnostic medical device). The real distribution and adoption path is institutional (ASHA workers, Primary Health Centres, disaster-response agencies, employers of outdoor labor) as much as individual consumers– the app must serve both a single wearer and someone monitoring many people at once. 2 Part I — Complete Feature List (Prioritized, 80/20 Principle) This section is written so that any team member can read it top to bottom and be able to explain the entire app to a mentor or judge in five minutes, without touching code. The very first experience– must work for a first-time smartphone user with no assumed literacy. UI/UX specification. ˆ ˆ ˆ ˆ Screen speaks its own instructions aloud on load, in the language selected on the very first tap (large flags/scripts, not a dropdown list). Vulnerability profile step: a grid of 6–8 large icon cards (elderly person, outdoor worker, pregnant, heart condition, child, none-of-these)– user taps as many as apply. No text typing required at all for this step. Emergency contact step uses the native contact picker, never a manual phone-number text field– typing a phone number correctly on a small keyboard is a common failure point for elderly users. A single “Calibrating your normal...” screen with a simple animated progress ring while the app collects ˜60 seconds of resting baseline data– explained as “We’re learning what’s normal for your body,” never using the word “baseline” or any technical term in-app. Why this design, for India specifically. Manual form-filling is the single biggest drop-off point for low-literacy and first-time smartphone users. Every step above replaces typing with tapping a picture or using a built-in phone capability (contacts) the user already understands from making calls. 4.2 Home / Status Screen [P1– CORE DEMO FEATURE] What it is. The one screen a user should be able to glance at and understand in under three seconds– this is the screen a judge will remember. UI/UX specification. ˆ ˆ ˆ ˆ ˆ Top of screen, largest element: a single colored orb/card (green / yellow / red) with one short sentence written the way a person would say it out loud:– Green: “You’re doing fine. Stay hydrated, it’s a hot afternoon.”– Yellow: “Your heart rate is a bit high for resting. Sit somewhere shaded for a few minutes.”– Red: “This doesn’t look normal. Please sit down and consider calling for help.” Below the status sentence, in smaller/secondary typography: the actual numbers (HR, SpO2, temperature, AQI) for users who want detail– numbers are secondary information, never the headline. A persistent, small “Working Offline” / “Connected” badge in the header– always visible, never hidden in settings. A persistent, small lock-icon “On-device– nothing leaves your phone” badge near the status card. The SOS button is always visible as a floating element on this screen, not nested in a menu. 6Sanjeevni — App Feature & Design Specification SIH 2026 — Team BrajCoders Whythis design. Context-aware modes that activate automatically based on environmental thresholds, rather than the user having to seek them out. UI/UX specification. ˆ ˆ ˆ ˆ Auto-activating banner, not a menu item the user must find– when heat-index/AQI/public advisory thresholds are crossed, a banner appears at the top of the Home screen: “Heat Wave Alert Today.” Tapping it opens a single-screen daily plan, not a wall of data: “Best hours to work outside today: before 10am, after 5pm.” This reframes a temperature number into a schedule– the single most actionable transformation for an outdoor worker. Every disaster mode ships with a matching, ultra-short “What to do” Action Card: 3–4 bullet points maximum, large text, e.g., symptoms of heat stroke to watch for, what to drink, what to avoid, how to cool someone down while waiting for help.
